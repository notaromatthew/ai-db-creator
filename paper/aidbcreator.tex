\begin{abstract}
The design of a relational database schema and the population of its tables require expertise in data modelling, normalisation, and SQL, which prevents non-expert users (domain experts, researchers, small-business operators) from structuring and querying their own data. This paper presents \aidb, a research platform that combines Large Language Models (LLMs) with an interactive visual interface to generate, populate, and explore relational databases from heterogeneous documents (CSV, Excel, PDF, TXT, SQL) and natural-language descriptions. The system guides users through five phases: document ingestion, LLM-based schema generation (normalised tables, primary and foreign keys, constraints), database creation and migration, full-LLM population from the source documents with a deterministic recovery path, and a visual data environment with search, filtering, inline editing, CRUD operations, and multi-dialect export (SQLite, PostgreSQL, MySQL, MSSQL, Oracle). Backup-and-restore snapshots and operation-history metadata provide safety and traceability. Forty-five trained student raters reached confirmatory ordinal agreement on 3NF compliance ($\alpha=0.812$), whereas a separate panel of three experts agreed little ($\alpha=0.054$ overall). In an exploratory five-run comparison against a gold-schema baseline, full-LLM population lowers cell-level F1 by about 0.30 on the University and Hospital datasets and is comparable on Library. We also evaluate the effect of human-in-the-loop validation in a three-arm experiment with 102 valid cases, 34 per arm. AI+UI did not differ from Manual or AI-only on the multiplicity-adjusted primary quality outcomes, but it reduced mean task time by 13.50 minutes relative to Manual and by 2.12 minutes relative to AI-only.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} large language models; relational database generation; schema generation; data population; provenance; reproducibility; human-in-the-loop; non-expert users.

\section{Introduction}
Relational databases remain a central paradigm for structured data storage in business, research, and government. Their creation, however, requires a combination of skills (data modelling, normalisation theory, and SQL proficiency) that is rarely found outside specialised technical roles. Research on database usability has long documented why database systems are difficult for non-expert users~\citep{jagadish2007usable}. This gap creates friction: data that could be queried, related, and analysed remains locked in isolated documents, and the benefits of structured storage (integrity constraints, efficient joins, and standardised access) are lost.

Large Language Models (LLMs) now perform well on several tasks adjacent to database creation. They have shown strong results in code generation~\citep{chen2021evaluating}, text-to-SQL translation~\citep{yu2018spider}, schema matching~\citep{parciak2024schema}, and structured data extraction~\citep{narayan2022data}. Recent systems now address schema generation directly, and SQUiD generates and populates relational databases from unstructured text~\citep{wang2025schemaagent,sadia2025squid}. The remaining research opportunity is therefore not the existence of an end-to-end generator, but the evaluation of a heterogeneous-document workflow that exposes schema and data revision to non-expert users, records their interactions, and compares assisted creation with manual and fully automatic conditions.

This paper presents \aidb, a research platform designed to fill this gap. \aidb\ combines an LLM-based generation engine with a visual interactive interface to support users without database expertise through the entire lifecycle of database creation. The key contributions of this work are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An end-to-end pipeline} that transforms heterogeneous documents (CSV, XLSX, PDF, TXT, SQL) into a normalised relational database through a guided five-phase workflow: ingestion, schema generation, database creation, data population, and exploration.
  \item \textbf{A full-LLM-first population strategy with deterministic recovery} that separates schema inference from extraction. The model receives the generated schema and the document content and returns INSERT statements; a rule-based header matcher, with semantic LLM mapping for unresolved columns, runs only when the model returns no usable SQL. Each run records its extraction method and source hashes.
  \item \textbf{A multi-dialect export engine} supporting five SQL dialects (SQLite, PostgreSQL, MySQL, MSSQL, and Oracle) with automatic type mapping across the five dialects.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation framework} comprising three empirical sub-questions with quantitative metrics. The framework covers schema quality (RQ1), population accuracy (RQ2), and human-in-the-loop validation impact (RQ3), under the overarching manual-versus-assisted comparison (RQ0).
  \item \textbf{A reproducible research infrastructure} with full source code, a documentation suite, versioned artifacts, and run manifests that record model configuration and SHA-256 hashes of prompts, documents, and outputs.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews related work in LLMs for databases, schema generation, and human-in-the-loop tools. Sections~\ref{sec:system}, \ref{sec:workflow}, and~\ref{sec:prompting} describe the architecture, the user workflow, and the prompting strategy. Sections~\ref{sec:rq} and~\ref{sec:eval} define the research questions and the evaluation protocol. Sections~\ref{sec:rq2-results}, \ref{sec:rq1-results}, and~\ref{sec:rq0-rq3-results} report the observed results. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} gives implementation details, Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses threats to validity, and Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility} lists the reproducibility artifacts. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} interpret the findings and conclude.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{LLMs for database-related tasks}
The application of LLMs to database tasks has grown rapidly. The most mature area is text-to-SQL translation, where models translate natural-language questions into SQL queries. Benchmarks such as Spider~\citep{yu2018spider}, WikiSQL~\citep{zhong2017wikisql}, and BIRD~\citep{li2023bird} have driven progress. However, text-to-SQL assumes that the database schema already exists; it does not address schema design.

Schema matching, the identification of correspondences between columns of different tables, has also been studied with LLMs: using only element names and descriptions, an off-the-shelf model can help data engineers bootstrap the matching~\citep{parciak2024schema}. Such matchers operate on existing schemas rather than generating new ones.

Data cleaning and transformation have been explored through LLM-based approaches for entity resolution, missing-value imputation, and format standardisation~\citep{narayan2022data}. These techniques are relevant to the population phase of \aidb, where extracted values may need type coercion or deduplication.

\subsection{Schema generation from natural language}
The task of generating a database schema from a natural-language description has received less attention than text-to-SQL. SchemaAgent formalises Text2Schema and uses specialised agents to generate 3NF logical schemas from requirements~\citep{wang2025schemaagent}. SchemaBridge subsequently showed that human requirements often under-specify referential structure and proposed separate measures for table, attribute, domain, entity, and referential fidelity~\citep{chakrabarti2026schemabridge}.

Reverse-engineering approaches infer schemas from data sources~\citep{rahm2001schema}. These methods analyse the structure of CSV files or spreadsheets to propose column types and potential keys, but they cannot infer relationships across files or enforce normalisation beyond what is evident from the data layout.

SQUiD is the closest end-to-end comparison: it generates a schema and populates
relational tables from unstructured text using a neuro-symbolic pipeline
\citep{sadia2025squid}. SynSQL also synthesises relational instances, but uses
them to stress-test text-to-SQL systems rather than to support an end-user
database-creation workflow~\citep{habibollah2026synsql}. \aidb\ therefore does
not claim to be the first schema-and-population system. It differs in its
combination of heterogeneous CSV/XLSX/PDF/TXT inputs, an editable visual
workflow, LLM-first population with deterministic recovery, operational provenance, multi-dialect
export, and an observed three-arm study of manual, automatic, and interactive
creation.

\subsection{Human-in-the-loop database tools}
Visual database design tools have existed for decades: MySQL Workbench, ER/Studio, dbdiagram.io, and others provide graphical environments for entity-relationship modelling. These tools suit users who understand normalisation, cardinality, and key selection, but they assume the very expertise that \aidb\ aims to replace. For non-expert users, the difficulty lies in knowing what a well-designed schema is, more than in the graphical notation.

The human-in-the-loop paradigm has been studied in the context of interactive machine learning~\citep{fails2003interactive,amershi2014power}, where user feedback is used to refine model outputs. In the database domain, Potter's Wheel demonstrated an interactive data-cleaning workflow that tightly couples transformation and discrepancy detection~\citep{raman2001potter}. \aidb\ applies this paradigm to schema generation: the LLM proposes a schema, and the user can review, edit, add, or remove elements in an editing interface before the database is created.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation for structured data}
RAG~\citep{lewis2020rag} combines a generative model with indexed external memory. While most RAG applications focus on question answering over unstructured text, the paradigm is also applicable to structured data. In \aidb, the RAG concept appears in two forms. First, the schema generation prompt includes bounded document summaries as context. Second, the population prompt uses the generated table definitions as a structured target schema and the document text as the source from which values must be extracted. The platform has no retrieval index, so this is an implementation choice and not a claim of conceptual priority.

\noindent\textbf{Literature-search status.} A targeted search of arXiv, the ACL Anthology, and ACM-indexed results identified direct work on schema generation and end-to-end relational synthesis, including SchemaAgent, SQUiD, SchemaBridge, and SynSQL. The search is a targeted review, not a systematic literature review.

The frontend is built with React 18, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS, using Vite as the build tool. It communicates with the backend through a typed API client that wraps fetch calls. The project workspace covers the schema (viewer, chat assistant, diagram, export and import controls), the data (table viewer with inline editing, search, filters, and CSV export), natural-language and direct SQL queries, backup management, and the operation history. Around it, the application provides a project dashboard, public read-only examples, a shared-project view, settings, a benchmark page, an administration page, a researcher panel, the participant-study flow, and an expert-review page.

The backend is built with FastAPI (tested on Python 3.10--3.13) and uses SQLAlchemy for database interactions. It exposes RESTful endpoints under the \texttt{/api} prefix. The backend is organised into service modules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{schema\_service}: project CRUD, schema storage and retrieval, schema update with migration, and LLM-based schema generation.
  \item \textbf{document\_service} and \textbf{pdf\_ocr}: storage of uploaded files, bounded text summaries, and OCR for scanned PDFs.
  \item \textbf{chat\_service}: the conversational schema assistant.
  \item \textbf{population\_service}: LLM-first population, chunking, the deterministic recovery path, row insertion, and per-row provenance.
  \item \textbf{query\_service}: natural-language-to-SQL generation.
  \item \textbf{backup\_service}: database file snapshot creation, listing, and restoration.
  \item \textbf{metrics\_service} and \textbf{benchmark\_service}: schema metrics and model benchmarks.
  \item \textbf{interaction\_logger} and the research modules (\textbf{research\_manifest}, \textbf{research\_runs}, \textbf{research\_study}, \textbf{research\_stats}, \textbf{expert\_evaluation\_service}): sanitised event logging, run manifests, and study analysis.
\end{itemize}
The \texttt{sql\_importer} and \texttt{db\_export} modules under \texttt{core} handle multi-dialect SQL CREATE TABLE parsing and schema-only or full-database export to five SQL dialects. Long generations run as background tasks on Celery with Redis, and sign-in is delegated to Keycloak.

\subsection{Document parsing layer}
The document parser supports five file formats:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CSV}: uses Python's csv module; auto-detects delimiter and header row.
  \item \textbf{Excel (.xlsx)}: uses openpyxl; reads all sheets and concatenates text.
  \item \textbf{PDF}: uses PyMuPDF (fitz); extracts text page by page.
  \item \textbf{TXT}: direct UTF-8 text extraction.
  \item \textbf{SQL}: reads CREATE TABLE statements for schema import (not parsed for population).
\end{itemize}
Each parser implements a common interface and returns both the raw text and metadata (filename, type, row count for tabular formats). The extracted text is passed to the LLM as context for schema generation and population. When the Vertex provider is configured, a scanned PDF without extractable text is transcribed page by page with the multimodal model, up to a page limit and a cost cap, and the transcription is stored next to the file.

\subsection{Phase 1: Document ingestion}
The user creates a project and uploads documents. Supported formats are CSV, Excel, PDF, TXT, and SQL (for schema import). Each document is parsed and stored with metadata. The system displays a summary of uploaded documents with file type indicators.

\subsection{Phase 2: Schema generation}
The user triggers schema generation either through the chat interface (describing the desired database in natural language) or through the ``Quick Generate'' feature, which sends bounded document summaries (up to 5,000 characters per document in the current implementation) to the LLM with a structured prompt. The prompt instructs the LLM to:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the entities present in the documents and their attributes.
  \item Design a normalised relational schema aiming for 3NF.
  \item Define appropriate data types (INTEGER, TEXT, REAL, BOOLEAN, DATE, etc.).
  \item Identify primary keys (surrogate or natural).
  \item Identify foreign-key relationships between tables.
  \item Mark NOT NULL constraints where data implies mandatory values.
  \item Return the result as a JSON object.
\end{enumerate}

The LLM response is parsed into the internal \texttt{NormalizedSchema} model. The schema is then validated structurally (unique names, primary keys, consistent foreign keys, permitted types). If errors remain, the model is asked to correct them, up to two times, and the best candidate is kept together with a record of the attempts and any remaining errors. A response that cannot be parsed is returned as a user-visible error. The generated schema is displayed as a collection of table cards or as an entity-relationship diagram.

\subsection{Phase 2b: Human review and editing (optional)}
The user can review the generated schema and make changes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rename tables and columns.
  \item Add or remove columns.
  \item Change data types.
  \item Toggle primary-key and NOT NULL constraints.
  \item Add or remove foreign keys.
  \item Add or delete entire tables.
\end{itemize}
Changes are applied both to the in-memory schema and, after saving, to the physical SQLite database through migration (additive changes use ALTER TABLE; new tables are created). Each saved change creates a new schema version; earlier versions can be inspected and restored, and a restore creates a further version. The user can mark the schema as final. In the chat, a change proposed by the assistant stays pending until the user applies or dismisses it.

\subsection{Phase 3: Database creation}
The schema is materialised as a SQLite database. If the database already exists (e.g.\ after a previous generation), the system compares old and new schemas and applies migration: new tables are created, new columns are added with ALTER TABLE, and data in existing columns is preserved.

\subsection{Phase 4: Data population}
Population is LLM-first for every document type. The parsed content of all selected documents (tables in full, free text up to a configurable limit) is split into overlapping chunks, and the model receives the target schema together with each chunk. The prompt instructs it to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item output only SQLite \texttt{INSERT OR IGNORE} statements for all relevant tables;
  \item keep primary-key and foreign-key values consistent across related tables;
  \item format dates as ISO strings and escape quotes;
  \item never use NULL for columns marked NOT NULL.
\end{itemize}
The statements are validated and executed against a fresh SQLite database; a statement with more than one \texttt{VALUES} tuple is rejected and counted as failed. The input is bounded by a per-request character cap and a per-run chunk limit; text beyond them is truncated and a warning is recorded in the run metadata.

A rule-based fallback runs only when the model returns no usable SQL. For CSV and Excel sources it normalises headers and matches them to schema columns, sends only unresolved headers to the model for semantic mapping, and keeps each value tied to its source document and tabular coordinates. For unstructured content it asks the model to extract candidate rows for each table. This path produces the finest provenance, while the primary route records provenance at document level. Each run records its extraction method, source-document hashes, provider and model configuration, and per-table inserted, skipped, and failed counts. A backup is created before population to enable rollback.

\subsection{Phase 5: Data exploration and export}
The user can explore the populated data through the Data tab:
\begin{itemize}
  \item View each table with row counts.
  \item Search across all tables (global text search).
  \item Filter rows by column values.
  \item Edit cell values inline (with yellow highlighting for unsaved changes).
  \item Add new rows.
  \item Delete rows.
  \item Export table data as CSV.
\end{itemize}
The Export button supports:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Schema-only export (DDL) for all five dialects.
  \item Full-database export (DDL + INSERT statements) for all five dialects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Supporting features}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Guided workflow stepper}: a four-step indicator showing the current phase and completeness status.
  \item \textbf{Backup manager}: manual labelled backups, automatic pre-destructive-operation backups, restore to any snapshot.
  \item \textbf{Operation history}: timestamped log of schema generations, populations, imports, backups, restores, queries, and data mutations. Export endpoints return artefacts but are not currently recorded as interaction events.
  \item \textbf{Query builder}: natural-language-to-SQL generation or direct SQL input with execution, with a history of recent questions.
  \item \textbf{Sharing}: owners can invite editors and readers by email or create a public read-only link; roles are enforced on the server.
  \item \textbf{Background jobs}: long generations run as queued jobs that can be cancelled, and their progress survives a page reload.
  \item \textbf{Usage quotas}: AI calls are counted per user per day, with a global cap.
\end{itemize}

\section{LLM prompting strategy}
\label{sec:prompting}
The LLM is used as schema designer and as the primary data extractor. The deterministic mapper is a recovery path that runs only when the model returns no usable SQL.

\subsection{Schema generation prompt}
The schema generation prompt follows a template with four components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Role assignment}: ``You are a database design expert. Your task is to analyse the following documents and create a normalised relational database schema.''
  \item \textbf{Domain description}: extracted from the user's optional natural-language description or inferred from filenames.
  \item \textbf{Document content}: bounded summaries of the uploaded documents, concatenated with clear source delimiters.
  \item \textbf{Structural instructions}: explicit requirements for normalisation, key selection, type assignment, NOT NULL constraints, and JSON output format.
\end{enumerate}

The exact prompt template is versioned in the repository, and its SHA-256 hash is recorded in a register that a test compares with the source, so a changed prompt is detected. The output contract is versioned (\texttt{schema-json-v2}). The output is parsed as JSON and validated against the \texttt{NormalizedSchema} Pydantic model; when the standard parser fails, a repair step extracts a single embedded JSON object, and output that cannot be repaired is rejected without logging the raw text.

\subsection{Population prompt and recovery path}
The primary population prompt has two parts. The system message assigns the role of SQL population expert and states seven rules: output only SQLite \texttt{INSERT OR IGNORE} statements, populate all relevant tables, preserve primary-key and foreign-key values across tables, map document fields to schema columns, use ISO dates with escaped quotes, never use NULL for NOT NULL columns, and return raw SQL without markdown. The user message contains the target tables as \texttt{CREATE TABLE} statements with the declared relationships, followed by the document content.

The recovery path is used only when this call returns no SQL. A semantic column-mapping prompt covers headers the rule-based matcher could not resolve, and an unstructured-extraction prompt is generated per table from its definition and the document text.

\subsection{Error handling and retries}
LLM output is parsed through typed JSON or constrained SQL-processing paths. Schema output that fails structural validation is sent back to the model for up to two corrections, and output that cannot be parsed raises a user-visible error. Table-level extraction and mapping failures are logged and return no candidate rows for that path. Parser, validation and fallback outcomes are retained in run metadata for error analysis.

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}
\label{sec:rq}
The overarching research question and its three sub-questions are:

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ0 (overarching).} How can an LLM-based system with an interactive visual interface enable non-expert users to create normalised and populated relational databases starting from textual descriptions and heterogeneous documents, and how does this approach compare with a fully manual database creation process?

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ1: Schema quality.} What is the structural accuracy (normalisation level, key identification, relationship coverage) of a database schema produced by an LLM from a prompt and a set of sample documents?

\noindent\textit{Metrics:} percentage of tables in 3NF, F1 score on primary-key detection, F1 score on foreign-key detection, relationship coverage, expert rating (5-point Likert on five dimensions).

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ2: Population accuracy.} With what precision does the system extract and map data from heterogeneous documents (CSV, Excel, PDF, TXT) into the generated tables, and which error types are prevalent across document formats?

\noindent\textit{Metrics:} cell-level precision, cell-level recall, row-level accuracy, error-type distribution (missing value, wrong value, misplaced value, duplicate, extraneous).

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ3: Human-in-the-loop validation.} To what extent does an interactive dashboard for schema review and editing reduce residual schema and population errors compared with fully automatic generation?

\noindent\textit{Method:} three-arm controlled between-subjects experiment (Manual, AI-only, AI+UI). The prespecified RQ0 contrast is Manual vs. AI+UI; the RQ3 contrast is AI-only vs. AI+UI. Primary outcomes are schema quality and residual error rate; completion time and task success are secondary.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
The evaluation used the following prespecified hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1.} The LLM-generated schema achieves $>80\%$ normalisation (tables in 3NF) for simple datasets (2--3 tables), with degradation to $<60\%$ for complex datasets (6+ tables).
  \item \textbf{H2.} Cell-level precision exceeds 85\% for structured document types (CSV, Excel) and falls below 60\% for unstructured types (PDF, TXT).
  \item \textbf{H3.} Participants in the AI+UI condition produce schemas with significantly fewer residual errors ($p < 0.05$, Cohen's $d > 0.5$) than those in the AI-only condition.
\end{itemize}
H1 could not be tested in its prespecified form, because RQ1 was assessed with ordinal rater scores and not with table-level 3NF proportions stratified by complexity (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Datasets}
Three versioned synthetic datasets provide increasing relational complexity:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{University (simple):} three tables (\texttt{students},
  \texttt{courses}, and \texttt{enrollments}) with a many-to-many relationship
  resolved through the enrolment table.
  \item \textbf{Library (medium):} five tables (\texttt{categories},
  \texttt{members}, \texttt{books}, \texttt{loans}, and \texttt{fines}) with
  linked circulation and fine records.
  \item \textbf{Hospital (complex):} eight tables spanning patients, doctors,
  wards, appointments, treatments, medications, prescriptions, and invoices,
  with multiple foreign-key paths.
\end{itemize}

Each dataset contains:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item source documents in the formats presented to the system;
  \item a versioned gold-standard schema with tables, columns, types, keys,
  and constraints;
  \item a populated ground-truth SQLite database and evaluation specification.
\end{enumerate}

All three datasets include source documents, a gold schema, and a ground-truth SQLite database. The evaluation package implements automated cell-level comparison, type-aware normalisation, schema alignment, Wilson intervals, and discrepancy classification. Independent human validation and a consensus procedure were completed for all three gold standards.

\subsection{Participants}
The formative pilot contains five completed sessions and was used to verify the operating procedure. The main study contains 102 valid completed cases, with 34 cases in each of the Manual, AI-only, and AI+UI arms. Dataset assignment is balanced overall (34 cases for each dataset) and differs by at most one case within an arm.

\subsection{Experimental procedure (RQ0 and RQ3)}
The between-subjects experiment compares three conditions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Manual condition:} participants use a conventional visual relational-database tool and its documentation, but no generative AI, schema generator, or pre-built template. They create both schema and population from the same task materials and under the same time limit.
  \item \textbf{AI-only condition:} participants upload documents, trigger schema generation, and receive the final database without any opportunity to review or edit the schema. They can only view the result.
  \item \textbf{AI+UI condition:} participants upload documents, trigger schema generation, then use the full editing interface to review and modify the schema (rename columns, change types, add/remove tables, toggle constraints, edit data, delete rows) before finalising.
\end{itemize}

Procedure for each participant:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Informed consent} (5 min): read and sign the consent form.
  \item \textbf{Tutorial} (10 min): standardised video tutorial showing the interface and the task.
  \item \textbf{Task} (30 min max): create a database from the dataset assigned to them (University, Library, or Hospital) using the assigned condition.
  \item \textbf{Post-task debrief} (5 min): optional privacy-filtered feedback, a brief interview about the experience, and a reminder of withdrawal rights; no NASA-TLX or SUS questionnaire is administered.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics}

\subsubsection{RQ1: Schema quality metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item 3NF compliance rate: proportion of tables satisfying all three normal form conditions (atomic columns, no partial dependencies, no transitive dependencies). Verified through functional dependency analysis.
  \item Key F1: precision and recall of primary-key detection (which columns are marked PK) and foreign-key detection (which FK relationships are identified).
  \item Relationship coverage: proportion of expected foreign-key relationships (from gold standard) that appear in the generated schema.
  \item Human rating: each schema is rated on five dimensions (D1 3NF compliance, D2 naming quality, D3 constraint coverage, D4 relationship accuracy, and D5 domain alignment) using a 5-point Likert scale. Inter-rater agreement is measured with Krippendorff's alpha using ordinal distance~\citep{krippendorff2018content}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Raters.} The planned RQ1 protocol targeted three database experts. The first rating export instead contains 45 computer-science students who were studying databases, each rating all 30 schemas on five dimensions. They are reported as trained student raters, not as professional or academic database experts, and this deviation from the plan is retained explicitly. Database coursework supports task relevance; independence additionally requires documented individual, condition-blind administration without discussion or access to peers' scores. The effective response scale is Likert 1--5. The primary agreement statistic is ordinal Krippendorff's $\alpha$ with schema-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals. The 30-run matrix that generated the rating stimuli was recovered and linked directly to the anonymised mapping: the seed-42 code assignment was reproduced, and all 30 rendered structural bodies match the corresponding project \texttt{schema\_json} (tables, columns, constraints, and relationships). This recovered matrix is the primary linkage artifact for RQ1.

A separate panel was subsequently completed by the three planned sector experts. The study coordinator attested that the first two worked individually, without discussion or access to one another's scores, and were blind to the condition labels, and that the third rater was an independent expert. On 24 November 2026 the coordinator also confirmed that the third rater was blind to the condition labels. These procedural facts are recorded as attestations and were not inferred from the ratings or from an independent administration record. The panel is analysed separately from the 45 student raters.

\subsubsection{RQ2: Population accuracy metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Cell-level precision: $\frac{\text{correctly populated cells}}{\text{total populated cells}}$
  \item Cell-level recall: $\frac{\text{correctly populated cells}}{\text{total gold-standard cells}}$
  \item Row-level accuracy: $\frac{\text{fully correct rows}}{\text{total rows}}$
  \item Cell-level F1: the harmonic mean of cell-level precision and recall.
  \item Functional-query pass rate: the proportion of frozen functional queries whose answers on the generated database match those on the gold database.
  \item Error type distribution: for each error, classify as one of:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Missing value: cell is NULL in population but has a value in gold standard.
      \item Wrong value: cell has a different value.
      \item Misplaced value: correct value appears in the wrong column.
      \item Duplicate row: same row appears multiple times.
      \item Extraneous row: row exists in population but not in gold standard.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

Primary cell metrics are restricted to facts observable in the supplied source
documents. An audit found that the current gold databases also contain values
created only by dataset builders (eight unobserved university students; library
loan--book links and fines; hospital treatments, medications, prescriptions and
invoices). These are excluded from primary denominators and retained only in a
full-gold sensitivity analysis. A versioned functional-query suite evaluates
information preservation independently of synthetic keys. The original
document bundles mix non-equivalent facts across formats and contain no XLSX
variant; they are therefore not used for format comparisons. A separate
derived fixture represents the same 45 observable facts in CSV, XLSX, TXT, and
PDF.

\subsubsection{RQ3: Human-in-the-loop metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Schema quality: the blinded schema-quality rating on a 1--5 scale, given independently by two raters and resolved by consensus.
  \item Residual error rate: proportion of schema elements (tables, columns, keys) or data cells that remain incorrect after the participant's intervention (or after generation in the AI-only condition).
  \item Time on task: minutes spent from start to submission.
  \item Task success: whether the participant completed the task.
\end{itemize}

The reproducible entry point \texttt{backend/analyze\_available\_records.py}
validates the static study registers, links each participant to the blinded
outcome through \texttt{blind\_artifact\_id}, derives task duration, computes the
two prespecified contrasts, and applies Holm correction across the four
contrast-by-primary-outcome tests. Percentile bootstrap intervals use 5,000
resamples and seed 20261103.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
RQ0 and RQ3 use two prespecified contrasts (Manual versus AI+UI, and AI-only versus AI+UI) on the two primary outcomes, schema quality and residual error rate. Each test is reported with the mean difference, Cohen's $d$ with a percentile bootstrap interval (5,000 resamples, seed 20261103), and a Welch test, and the four primary tests are adjusted with Holm's procedure. Duration and task success are secondary. The benchmark protocol listed an independent-samples $t$-test with a Mann--Whitney backup; the final analysis scripts use Welch's test, which does not assume equal variances, together with the bootstrap intervals.

For RQ1, agreement is measured with ordinal Krippendorff's $\alpha$ and schema-clustered bootstrap intervals (5,000 resamples). The protocol's decision rule treats $\alpha\ge0.80$ as supporting confirmatory interpretation, $0.67\le\alpha<0.80$ as tentative, and lower values as descriptive only. For the condition comparison, ratings are averaged within schema so that the schema is the experimental unit, and the five dimension-level tests are adjusted with Holm's procedure.

RQ2 comparisons are exploratory and based on five repetitions per dataset and condition. They use exact permutation tests within each dataset with Holm adjustment across dataset-by-metric tests, Spearman correlation between cell-level F1 and functional-query pass rate, and Holm-corrected pairwise Fisher tests of technical success across formats. Wilson intervals are not used for run-level means.

\subsection{Backup service}
Backups are direct SQLite database-file snapshots accompanied by JSON metadata containing project ID, timestamp, label, filename and size. Before population, SQL import and write-query execution, the system creates an automatic snapshot. Restore first creates an undo snapshot and then replaces the active database file; it rejects backup names that would leave the backup directory. Research events are persisted as sanitised JSON records and exported per project. Each entry contains a run ID, project ID, event type, UTC timestamp and event-specific metadata. Secret-like fields and bearer-token patterns are redacted at the logging boundary; raw credentials are never part of research exports.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Internal validity}
The primary internal threat is the quality of the gold standard. If the gold-standard schemas or population data contain errors, all metrics will be biased. Two independent experts reviewed each gold standard and resolved discrepancies through a recorded consensus procedure. Their signed qualification and independence forms were archived after the technical consensus and are therefore retrospective attestations rather than contemporaneous pre-review records.

A second threat is LLM non-determinism. The same prompt may produce different schemas or populations across runs. Mitigation: exposed decoding parameters are fixed per task (schema generation and primary population use temperature $0.1$ by default, and the recovery path's column mapping and extraction use $0.0$), exact configuration and input hashes are logged, and repeated runs are reported as distributions. Provider-side hidden revisions remain a reproducibility limitation.

A further threat concerns comparability over time. The population route was inverted during development, from deterministic-first to LLM-first (recorded in the CHANGELOG on 3 August 2026). Runs recorded under the earlier order are not comparable with later ones and must not be pooled.

A third threat is contamination: the models may have encountered public examples of the library, university, and hospital domains during training, which could inflate both baseline and full-LLM scores. A fourth concerns sensitivity to the prompt and dependence on few models: the RQ2 matrices use two models (Gemini and a free OpenRouter model), one set of prompt templates, and five repetitions per cell, so the results should not be generalised to other models or prompts. Finally, inputs longer than the per-request character cap and the per-run chunk limit are truncated, which can lower recall on long documents; each truncation is recorded as a warning in the run metadata.

\subsection{External validity}
The participant sample may not represent the full population of non-expert users. The document sets are artificial and may not capture the messiness and variability of real-world business data. Replication studies with real-world datasets and broader participant pools are needed.

\subsection{Construct validity}
Schema quality is a multidimensional construct. A single metric (e.g.\ 3NF compliance) captures only one aspect. The rating rubric attempts to cover multiple dimensions, but judgment is inherently subjective. The 45 RQ1 raters are trained students rather than professional database experts, and the independence of both rater groups rests on attestations (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). The current protocol intentionally omits perceived workload and usability questionnaires, so it cannot support claims about cognitive load or perceived usability; these remain possible outcomes for a separate future revision.

\subsection{Conclusion validity}
The achieved sample meets the target of 34 completed participants per arm. The four primary tests comprise two prespecified contrasts across schema quality and residual error rate and use Holm correction. Completion time and task success are secondary outcomes.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
The analysis is based on the available completed records.

The reproducibility package versions the following artifacts:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source code}: versioned source code of the frontend and backend.
  \item \textbf{Datasets}: three gold-standard datasets with schema, functional dependencies and population.
  \item \textbf{LLM prompts}: exact prompt templates are versioned in the source tree.
  \item \textbf{Experiment scripts}: frozen automated evaluation scripts for RQ1 and RQ2.
  \item \textbf{Analysis code}: frozen scripts for RQ0 and RQ3, including the available-record analysis and its deterministic manifest.
  \item \textbf{Study registers}: static pilot, participant, and blinded-outcome workbooks used by the participant analysis.
  \item \textbf{Run manifests}: run ID, experimental condition, exact provider/model identifier, decoding parameters, prompt-template version, hashes of prompts/documents, software revision, timestamps, extraction method, and before/after schema hashes. Credentials and raw sensitive content are excluded.
  \item \textbf{Provenance artifacts}: for each inserted row, the source documents and the target table, columns, and outcome. The deterministic recovery path additionally records sheet or page, row and column coordinates, mapping method, and confidence where available, whereas the primary path records provenance at document level.
\end{itemize}

For the completed RQ1 and RQ2 evidence, a machine-readable public index
classifies ten components, records SHA-256 hashes for 44 selected artifacts,
and verifies 14 internal output hashes. The index preserves
the distinction between the primary 38/40 format matrix and the secondary
post-outcome 40/40 recovery overlay.

The tested Python compatibility target is 3.10--3.13 with Node.js 18+. Python 3.14 is not currently supported by the pinned Pydantic/PyO3 dependency chain. Cloud-provider reproduction requires a researcher-supplied API key and may incur provider-dependent cost; no assumption is made about continuing free-tier availability. Results from different providers or model versions are analysed as distinct conditions.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Main findings}
The results separate structural quality from operational efficiency. RQ1 shows
that generated schemas can receive high ratings while agreement among raters
remains limited. This reinforces the need to report both central tendency and
inter-rater reliability rather than treating an expert score as an objective
ground truth. The independent three-expert panel reaches the planned size but
does not erase the observed disagreement. H1 is not confirmed in its original
threshold form because the observed RQ1 evidence uses ordinal D1 ratings rather
than table-level 3NF proportions stratified by complexity. The positive D1
contrast therefore supports a relative structural-quality finding but not the
specific percentage thresholds stated in H1.

RQ2 likewise shows that cell-level similarity and functional adequacy are not
interchangeable. The structured-output comparison finds dataset-dependent F1
differences, while the functional-query results are less decisive. The format
experiment does not identify a performance difference among successful CSV,
XLSX, TXT, and PDF runs, but the two retained generation failures show that
completion reliability remains part of the end-to-end outcome.
H2 is not supported as a format-dependent accuracy gradient: among successful
runs on the controlled fixture, accuracy is identical across CSV, XLSX, TXT,
and PDF within each condition. This null format result is limited to one
fixture and five repetitions per cell.

For RQ0, the assisted workflow does not improve the two primary quality
outcomes relative to Manual after multiplicity correction. It does, however,
reduce mean completion time by 13.50 minutes while preserving 100\% task
success. The result supports an efficiency claim and gives no evidence of superior
quality. The very large standardised duration effect should be interpreted in
the context of the narrow within-arm time variation in the available records.

For RQ3, AI+UI and AI-only also have indistinguishable primary outcomes. H3 is
therefore not supported. AI+UI is 2.12 minutes faster on average, suggesting
that the interface may streamline completion without producing a measurable
quality gain under the present tasks. A ceiling effect is plausible: mean
schema quality is at least 4.97 in all arms, residual error is approximately
2\%, and every case succeeds. Harder tasks or more discriminating outcome
measures may be required to expose a human-review advantage.

These findings refine the design implication of the platform. The strongest
observed benefit is a faster end-to-end workflow at comparable final quality;
the data give no sign that the interface repairs substantially worse AI
output.

\subsection{Positioning with respect to related work}
SchemaAgent and SchemaBridge study schema generation from requirements~\citep{wang2025schemaagent,chakrabarti2026schemabridge}, and SQUiD generates and populates relational tables from unstructured text~\citep{sadia2025squid}. Their datasets, inputs, and metrics differ from those used here, so the results are not numerically comparable. The present study evaluates a workflow that accepts heterogeneous tabular and unstructured documents, exposes schema and data revision to non-expert users, and compares assisted creation with manual and fully automatic conditions.

\subsection{Limitations}
The principal validity constraints are the artificial document sets, the
restricted participant context, provider-side model evolution, and the strong
ceiling in the controlled outcomes. The study does not measure perceived
workload or usability because NASA-TLX and SUS were excluded from the current
protocol. Consequently, the paper limits its claims to observed structural,
population, task-success, and timing outcomes.

\subsection{Future work}
Future studies can test whether the efficiency finding persists with broader participant populations, noisier business documents, larger schemas, and provider or model changes. A controlled ablation of the deterministic recovery path against the LLM-first route would also show what the inversion of the population order contributes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented \aidb, a research platform that combines LLM-based generation with an interactive visual interface to enable non-expert users to create normalised, populated relational databases from heterogeneous documents. We described the architecture, workflow, prompting strategy, and an evaluation spanning the overarching manual comparison and three empirical sub-questions.

The evaluation combines complementary evidence on schema quality (RQ1), population accuracy (RQ2), and human review (RQ3). RQ1 includes the trained-student analysis and a separate three-expert panel. RQ2 includes the complete 30-run structured-output sensitivity, blinded adjudication of 1,294 discrepancy records, and a separate 40-run format matrix with 38 primary successes and two retained failures. The three-arm participant analysis includes 102 valid cases. It finds no multiplicity-adjusted difference in schema quality or residual error, but AI+UI completes the task faster than both Manual and AI-only while maintaining 100\% task success.

The complete source code, evaluation artifacts, static study registers, and
analysis outputs support replication and extension of the reported results.
